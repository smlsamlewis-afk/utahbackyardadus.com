A growing share of the ADUs we build aren't for rental income at all, they're for family: aging parents who want independence without distance, adult children saving toward their own home, or households simply choosing to live near each other by design. Building for this purpose well requires some different thinking than a standard rental unit.

Why Utah Families Are Building Multigenerational ADUs

Rising home prices along the Wasatch Front have made multigenerational living a practical choice, not just a cultural preference, for a lot of families. An ADU offers something a shared house often can't: real privacy and independence for both households, while keeping everyone on the same property for caregiving, childcare, or simply staying close.

Design Features That Matter Most

If aging in place is a real consideration, even years down the road, a handful of design choices are far easier to build in from day one than to retrofit later:

  • Zero-step entry — no stairs at the main entrance, which matters immediately for wheelchair or walker access and eventually for anyone with mobility changes
  • Wider doorways and hallways — 36-inch doorways and adequately wide hallways accommodate mobility devices without a future remodel
  • Roll-in or curbless showers — far easier and cheaper to build correctly the first time than to retrofit a standard tub-shower combo later
  • Lever-style door handles and single-lever faucets — small details that matter a lot for anyone with limited grip strength
  • Single-story layout — most ADUs are single story by nature, which is itself one of the biggest aging-in-place advantages over a multi-level home
  • Good lighting and slip-resistant flooring — especially in bathrooms and entryways

Universal Design vs. Full ADA Compliance

It's worth clarifying a common point of confusion: the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) technically applies to public accommodations and commercial spaces, not private single-family homes. What most homeowners actually want is "universal design," a set of best practices borrowed from accessibility standards that make a home more comfortable and usable for people of all ages and ability levels, without necessarily meeting the full technical requirements of commercial ADA compliance. In practice, this gives you flexibility to prioritize the features that matter most for your specific family rather than checking every box on a commercial accessibility code.

Planning for the Future, Not Just Today

The families who are happiest with their multigenerational ADU years later are usually the ones who designed a little ahead of their immediate needs: a parent who's independent and mobile today but chose a zero-step entry anyway, or a family that built a wider bathroom doorway "just in case" long before it was actually needed. These choices add very little to the cost during initial construction and are dramatically more expensive to add after the fact.
